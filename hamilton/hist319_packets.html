HIST 319 · Three Voices on the Civil War

Team source packets

The old texts for every team, as printed, with a link to each original page. Pick your team, read the context card, then the excerpt. Copy with step 2 prompt puts the step 2 prompt, your author and your excerpt on the clipboard, ready to paste into your model.

How to read your packet

Read the context card before the excerpt. Who wrote it, when, and for which audience will tell you more than any single sentence. Then read the excerpt once straight through, and only after that paste it into the modern AI.

  • Every excerpt was printed before 1929 and is in the public domain. The spelling and punctuation are the original's. "[…]" marks a cut; words in square brackets are not the author's.
  • The excerpts were transcribed from scanned books. If a word looks odd, check the page image at the link under each excerpt.
  • Some passages use the racial language of their time, and several state white supremacy outright. They are printed here as evidence of what was said, not as views anyone in this class shares.

Opening: one meeting, two versions

Harriet Beecher Stowe, author of Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852), met President Lincoln at the White House in late 1862. Neither of them left a record of what he said. Here is the greeting as two books told it, fourteen years apart.

Version AAnnie Fields, Life and Letters of Harriet Beecher Stowe (1897), pp. 268–269

Fields was a friend of Stowe's and the widow of James T. Fields, the Boston publisher and former editor of the Atlantic Monthly.

It was left for others to speak of Mrs. Stowe's interview with President Lincoln. Her daughter was told that when the President heard her name he seized her hand, saying, "Is this the little woman who made this great war?" He then led her to a seat in the window, where they were withdrawn, and undisturbed by other guests.

Original: pp. 268–269, Internet Archive
Version BCharles Edward Stowe and Lyman Beecher Stowe, Harriet Beecher Stowe: The Story of Her Life (1911), pp. 202–203

The authors were her son and her grandson.

Mrs. Stowe, in telling of her interview with Lincoln at this time, dwelt particularly on the rustic pleasantry with which that great man received her. She was introduced into a cosy room where the President had been seated before an open fire, for the day was damp and chilly. It was Mr. Seward who introduced her, and Mr. Lincoln rose awkwardly from his chair, saying, "Why, Mrs. Stowe, right glad to see you!" Then with a humorous twinkle in his eye, he said, "So you're the little woman who wrote the book that made this great war! Sit down, please," he added, as he seated himself once more before the fire […].

Original: pp. 202–203, Internet Archive

Compare

  • Who is the source of the story in A, and in B?
  • What details appear in B that are not in A?
  • Which version do you hear quoted today, and why might that be?

Team 1Robert E. Lee

You have two texts, forty-two years apart. One of you takes each, then trade.

Context card ARobert E. Lee, General Order No. 9 (April 10, 1865)

Author
General Robert E. Lee, commander of the Confederate Army of Northern Virginia. His aide, Col. Charles Marshall, drafted it and Lee revised it.
Occasion
Issued the day after Lee surrendered to Grant at Appomattox Court House. Many copies were written out by hand; the text here follows one held at Indiana University's Lilly Library.
Audience
Lee's own soldiers, about to go home
Excerpt Athe order, without its heading

After four years of arduous service marked by unsurpassed courage and fortitude, the Army of Northern Virginia has been compelled to yield to overwhelming numbers and resources.

I need not tell the brave survivors of so many hard fought battles who have remained steadfast to the last that I have consented to this result from no distrust of them; but feeling that valour and devotion could accomplish nothing that would compensate for the loss that would have attended the continuance of the contest, I determined to avoid the useless sacrifice of those whose past services have endeared them to their Countrymen.

By the terms of the Agreement officers and men can return to their homes and remain there until exchanged.

You will take with you the satisfaction that proceeds from the consciousness of duty faithfully performed, and I earnestly pray that a Merciful God will extend to you his blessing and protection.

With an unceasing admiration of your constancy and devotion to your country and a grateful remembrance of your kind and generous consideration for myself, I bid you all an affectionate farewell.

R. E. Lee
Genl

Original: manuscript copy and transcription, Lilly Library

Context card BCharles Francis Adams, Lee's Centennial (1907)

Author
Charles Francis Adams Jr. (1835–1915), grandson of President John Quincy Adams and president of the Massachusetts Historical Society
In the war
A Union cavalry officer who fought against Lee's army
Occasion
An address at Washington and Lee University in Lexington, Virginia, on January 19, 1907, the 100th anniversary of Lee's birth. Lee had been the university's president after the war and is buried there.
Audience
Southern listeners, invited by the university
Excerpt B1pp. 4–5

One of a community which then looked upon Lee as a renegade from the flag he had sworn to serve, and a traitor to the Nation which had nurtured him, in my subordinate place I directly confronted Lee throughout the larger portion of the War of Secession. During all those years there was not a day in which my heart would not have been gladdened had I heard that his also had been the fate which at Chancellorsville befell his great lieutenant; and yet more glad had it been the fortune of the command in which I served to visit that fate upon him. Forty more years have since gone. Their close finds me here to-day — certainly a much older, and, in my own belief at least, a wiser man. Nay, more! A distinguished representative of Massachusetts, speaking in the Senate of the United States shortly after Lee's death upon the question of a return to Lee's family of the ancestral estate of Arlington, used these words: "Eloquent Senators have already characterized the proposition and the traitor it seeks to commemorate. I am not disposed to speak of General Lee. It is enough to say he stands high in the catalogue of those who have imbrued their hands in their country's blood. I hand him over to the avenging pen of History." […] a whole generation having passed away since Charles Sumner uttered the words I have quoted, I do, on your invitation, chance to stand here to-day, as I have said, both a Massachusetts man and the head of the Massachusetts Historical Society, to pass judgment upon General Lee.

Original: pp. 4–5, Internet Archive
Excerpt B2p. 8

Coming directly to the point, I maintain that every man in the eleven States seceding from the Union had in 1861, whether he would or no, to decide for himself whether to adhere to his State or to the Nation; and I finally assert that, whichever way he decided, if only he decided honestly, putting self-interest behind him, he decided right.

Original: p. 8, Internet Archive
Excerpt B3pp. 20–21

It may have been treason to take this position; the man who took it, uttering these words and sacrificing as he sacrificed, may have been technically a renegade to his flag, — if you please, false to his allegiance; but he stands awaiting sentence at the bar of history in very respectable company. […] As to Robert E. Lee, individually, I can only repeat what I have already said, — if in all respects similarly circumstanced, I hope I should have been filial and unselfish enough to have done as Lee did." Such an utterance on my part may be "traitorous;" but I here render that homage.

Original: pp. 20–21, Internet Archive

Read for

  • Lee explains the defeat in his first sentence. What reason does he give, and what does he leave out? Team 2 will find the same reason in Jubal Early's speech of 1872.
  • Adams sets two judgments side by side: the senator's in the 1870s and his own in 1907. What changed in between, and what does Adams say changed?
  • In Excerpt B2, what does Adams make the war's question? What word never appears?
  • Does talkie sound more like Lee, the senator, or Adams?

Check

Adams says the "avenging pen of History" words were Charles Sumner's. Ask the modern AI whether Sumner said them and when, then check its answer in a source you can cite.

Team 2Ulysses S. Grant

You have two short texts that see Grant from opposite sides. One of you takes each, then trade.

Context card AJubal A. Early, address on Lee (1872)

Author
Jubal A. Early (1816–1894), a Confederate lieutenant general under Lee. After the war he became one of the chief writers of the "Lost Cause" account of the war.
Occasion
An address at Washington and Lee University on January 19, 1872, Lee's birthday, fifteen months after Lee's death
Audience
Former Confederates and the university community
Excerpt App. 44–45

Finally, from mere exhaustion, less than eight thousand men, with arms in their hands, of the noblest army that had ever fought, "in all the tide of time," were surrendered at Appamattox to an army of one hundred and fifty thousand men […]. Who is it that stands out the grandest figure in that last sad scene of the drama? Is it the victor? Victor over what? […] General Lee had not been conquered in battle, but surrendered because he had no longer an army with which to give battle. What he surrendered was the skeleton, the mere ghost of the Army of Northern Virginia, which had been gradually worn down by the combined agencies of numbers, steam-power, railroads, mechanism, and all the resources of physical science.

Original: pp. 44–45, Internet Archive

Context card BFrederick Douglass, Life and Times of Frederick Douglass (1881)

Author
Frederick Douglass (about 1818–1895), who escaped slavery, became the leading Black abolitionist, and recruited Black soldiers for the Union army
Occasion
His third autobiography, written after Reconstruction had ended
Audience
A national readership, Black and white
Excerpt Bp. 363

This confidence was immeasurably strengthened when I saw Gen. George B. McClellan relieved from the command of the army of the Potomac and Gen U. S. Grant placed at its head, and in command of all the armies of the United States. My confidence in Gen. Grant was not entirely due to the brilliant military successes achieved by him, but there was a moral as well as military basis for my faith in him. He had shown his single mindedness and superiority to popular prejudice by his prompt co-operation with President Lincoln in his policy of employing colored troops, and his order commanding his soldiers to treat such troops with due respect. In this way he proved himself to be not only a wise General, but a great man—one who could adjust himself to new conditions, and adopt the lessons taught by the events of the hour.

Original: p. 363, Internet Archive

Read for

  • In Excerpt A, Grant is never named. Who wins the scene instead, and how does Early explain the defeat?
  • What does Douglass praise Grant for that Early's story leaves out entirely?
  • When you ask talkie about Grant, whose account does it echo, Early's or Douglass's?

Check

Early says fewer than eight thousand men surrendered. The National Park Service counts 28,231 Confederate paroles issued at Appomattox. Ask the modern AI how many surrendered, then work out what Early counted and what he left out.

Team 3Reconstruction

After the war, Congress gave Black men the vote. From 1867, Southern states wrote new constitutions and elected legislatures with Black members, and by 1877 white Democrats had taken back every Southern state government. Three texts from 1905 to 1910 tell that story in opposite ways. One of you takes A and B, the other takes C, then trade.

Context card AThomas Dixon Jr., The Clansman: An Historical Romance of the Ku Klux Klan (1905)

Author
Thomas Dixon Jr. (1864–1946), a North Carolina Baptist minister, lecturer and best-selling novelist
Form
A novel, opened by a preface addressed "To the Reader"
What came next
Dixon turned it into a stage play in 1905, and D. W. Griffith filmed it as The Birth of a Nation (1915)
Excerpt A"To the Reader," dated December 14, 1904

"The Clansman" develops the true story of the "Ku Klux Klan Conspiracy," which overturned the Reconstruction régime. […]

The chaos of blind passion that followed Lincoln's assassination is inconceivable to-day. The Revolution it produced in our Government, and the bold attempt of Thaddeus Stevens to Africanise ten great states of the American Union, read now like tales from "The Arabian Nights."

I have sought to preserve in this romance both the letter and the spirit of this remarkable period. The men who enact the drama of fierce revenge into which I have woven a double love-story are historical figures. I have merely changed their names without taking a liberty with any essential historic fact.

In the darkest hour of the life of the South, when her wounded people lay helpless amid rags and ashes under the beak and talon of the Vulture, suddenly from the mists of the mountains appeared a white cloud the size of a man's hand. It grew until its mantle of mystery enfolded the stricken earth and sky. An "Invisible Empire" had risen from the field of Death and challenged the Visible to mortal combat.

How the young South, led by the reincarnated souls of the Clansmen of Old Scotland, went forth under this cover and against overwhelming odds, daring exile, imprisonment, and a felon's death, and saved the life of a people, forms one of the most dramatic chapters in the history of the Aryan race.

Original: "To the Reader," Internet Archive

Context card BWilliam A. Dunning, Reconstruction, Political and Economic, 1865–1877 (1907)

Author
William Archibald Dunning (1857–1922), professor of history at Columbia University and president of the American Historical Association in 1913. His students' books on Reconstruction in each Southern state became known as the "Dunning School."
Form
A volume in The American Nation: A History, a multi-volume series edited by Albert Bushnell Hart of Harvard
Audience
Students and general readers; it stayed a standard account for decades
Excerpt B1p. 213

What animated the whites was pride in their race as such and a dread, partly instinctive, partly rational, lest their institutions, traditions, and ideals were to be appropriated or submerged. Whether or not this feeling and spirit were abstractly preferable to those which animated the northern idealist who preached equality, the fact that such feeling and spirit were at work must be taken squarely into account by the historian.

The negro had no pride of race and no aspiration or ideals save to be like the whites. With civil rights and political power, not won, but almost forced upon him, he came gradually to understand and crave those more elusive privileges that constitute social equality.

Original: p. 213, Internet Archive
Excerpt B2p. 341, the book's closing words

Generalized, this famous bargain [the 1877 agreement that settled the disputed election in favor of Rutherford B. Hayes] meant: Let the reforming Republicans direct the national government and the southern whites may rule the negroes. Such were the terms on which the new administration took up its task. They precisely and consciously reversed the principles of reconstruction as followed under Grant, and hence they ended an era. Grant in 1868 had cried peace, but in his time, with the radicals and carpet-baggers in the saddle, there was no peace; with Hayes peace came.

Original: p. 341, Internet Archive

Context card CW. E. B. Du Bois, "Reconstruction and Its Benefits," American Historical Review 15, no. 4 (July 1910): 781–799

Author
W. E. B. Du Bois (1868–1963), then a professor at Atlanta University and the first Black American to earn a Harvard PhD (1895)
Occasion
A paper read at the American Historical Association's annual meeting in New York in December 1909, then printed in the profession's leading journal
Audience
Professional historians, most of whom accepted the Dunning School account
Excerpt C1p. 781, the opening

There is danger to-day that between the intense feeling of the South and the conciliatory spirit of the North grave injustice will be done the negro American in the history of Reconstruction. Those who see in negro suffrage the cause of the main evils of Reconstruction must remember that if there had not been a single freedman left in the South after the war the problems of Reconstruction would still have been grave.

Original: p. 781, Internet Archive
Excerpt C2p. 799, the closing

Reconstruction constitutions, practically unaltered, were kept in
Florida, 1868–1885 17 years.
Virginia, 1870–1902 32 years.
South Carolina, 1868–1895 27 years.
Mississippi, 1868–1890 22 years. […]

Paint the "carpet-bag" governments and negro rule as black as may be, the fact remains that the essence of the revolution which the overturning of the negro governments made was to put these black men and their friends out of power. Outside the curtailing of expenses and stopping of extravagance, not only did their successors make few changes in the work which these legislatures and conventions had done, but they largely carried out their plans, followed their suggestions, and strengthened their institutions. Practically the whole new growth of the South has been accomplished under laws which black men helped to frame thirty years ago. I know of no greater compliment to negro suffrage.

Original: p. 799, Internet Archive

Read for

  • Dixon calls his novel a "true story," and Dunning was a Columbia professor. What does each say about the Black voters and officeholders of Reconstruction?
  • Du Bois spoke to the same profession that taught Dunning's account. What does he say historians are getting wrong, and why "to-day"?
  • Ask talkie about Reconstruction. Does it sound like Dixon and Dunning, or like Du Bois? Five years after talkie's cutoff, Du Bois answered the Dunning School at book length in Black Reconstruction in America (1935).

Check

Du Bois says several states kept their Reconstruction constitutions for decades: Mississippi until 1890, South Carolina until 1895, Virginia until 1902. Ask the modern AI whether those dates are right and what the new constitutions changed for Black voters. Then check one answer in a source you can cite.

Team 4What caused the war

Three texts, one for each of you. The first two are by the same man, seven years apart. Read your own closely, then read the other two quickly.

Context card AAlexander H. Stephens, the "Cornerstone Speech" (March 21, 1861)

Speaker
Alexander H. Stephens (1812–1883) of Georgia, Vice President of the Confederacy
Occasion
A speech at the Athenaeum in Savannah, three weeks before the attack on Fort Sumter, explaining the new Confederate constitution
How it survives
Only as a newspaper reporter's account, reprinted in 1866. The reporter added a note warning it was not a perfect report.
Excerpt ACleveland, ed., 1866, pp. 721 and 729

The new constitution has put at rest, forever, all the agitating questions relating to our peculiar institution—African slavery as it exists amongst us—the proper status of the negro in our form of civilization. This was the immediate cause of the late rupture and present revolution. […] Our new government is founded upon exactly the opposite idea; its foundations are laid, its corner-stone rests upon the great truth, that the negro is not equal to the white man; that slavery—subordination to the superior race—is his natural and normal condition. [Applause.]

This, our new government, is the first, in the history of the world, based upon this great physical, philosophical, and moral truth. […]

[Reporter's Note.—Your reporter begs to state that the above is not a perfect report, but only such a sketch of the address of Mr. Stephens as embraces, in his judgment, the most important points presented by the orator.—G.]

Original: p. 721 and p. 729, Internet Archive

Context card BAlexander H. Stephens, A Constitutional View of the Late War Between the States, vol. 1 (1868)

Author
The same Stephens, writing after defeat and five months' imprisonment at the war's end
Form
A two-volume book written as conversations at Liberty Hall, his home in Georgia
Audience
A national readership, three years after the war
Excerpt Bvol. 1, pp. 9–10, Introduction

It is a postulate, with many writers of this day, that the late War was the result of two opposing ideas, or principles, upon the subject of African Slavery. Between these, according to their theory, sprung the "irrepressible conflict," in principle, which ended in the terrible conflict of arms. Those who assume this postulate, and so theorize upon it, are but superficial observers. […] The conflict in principle arose from different and opposing ideas as to the nature of what is known as the General Government. The contest was between those who held it to be strictly Federal in its character, and those who maintained that it was thoroughly National. It was a strife between the principles of Federation, on the one side, and Centralism, or Consolidation, on the other.

Slavery, so called, was but the question on which these antagonistic principles, which had been in conflict, from the beginning, on divers other questions, were finally brought into actual and active collision with each other on the field of battle.

Original: pp. 9–10, Internet Archive

Context card CMildred Lewis Rutherford, Wrongs of History Righted (1914)

Author
Mildred Lewis Rutherford (1851–1928) of Athens, Georgia, Historian General of the United Daughters of the Confederacy from 1911 to 1916
Occasion
An address delivered in Savannah, Georgia, on November 13, 1914, then printed as a pamphlet
Audience
Members of the United Daughters of the Confederacy, women descended from Confederate soldiers
Later
In 1919 she wrote A Measuring Rod to Test Text Books for the Confederate veterans, telling schools and libraries which history books to reject
Excerpt C1p. 3, the opening

My object this evening is not to stress the omissions of history, but rather to urge that some of the wrongs that have already entered history be righted. […]

When sons and daughters of Veterans write articles for newspapers and magazines, condemning the principles for which their Confederate fathers fought, and even stand for a changed Constitution that will overthrow the very bulwark of the South—state sovereignty—it is full time for the Daughters of the Confederacy and Veterans to become insistent that the truths of history shall be written, and that those truths shall be correctly taught in our schools and colleges.

So long as we send our Southern boys to Harvard to be taught "The Essentials of American History" by Dr. Albert Bushnell Hart, so long may we expect them to question the principles for which their fathers fought.

Original: p. 3, Internet Archive
Excerpt C2pp. 13–14

Was the war fought to hold our slaves? Ah! how often have we of the South had this cast into our teeth and often by some of our own southern people. Yes, it is full time this wrong should be righted. […]

No, the war was not fought to hold slaves, but a few selfish Southern people may have thought so.

Gen. Grant said, "If I thought this war was to abolish slavery, I would resign my commission and offer my sword to the other side." The North had no thought of fighting to abolish slaves, then why should the South be troubled on that score?

Original: pp. 13–14, Internet Archive

Read for

  • In 1861 Stephens names the "immediate cause." In 1868, what does he call people who say the same thing?
  • By 1914, what does Rutherford call the claim that the South fought to keep slavery? Who does she want to decide what Southern students are taught?
  • Ask talkie what caused the war. Which of the three texts does its answer match?

Check

Rutherford quotes General Grant: "If I thought this war was to abolish slavery, I would resign my commission and offer my sword to the other side." Ask the modern AI where this quotation first appeared and whether it is in Grant's own letters or memoirs. Then check its answer in a source you can cite. Team 2 is studying Grant, so compare notes in your mixed group.
