04

How LLMs are built

How Large Language Models are Built

  1. Collect text. For talkie: books, newspapers, journals, patents and court decisions printed before 1931. For today's models: text gathered from the web and many other sources.
  2. Cut it into tokens. A token is a word or a piece of a word.
  3. Train. The model reads the tokens and adjusts billions of numbers, its parameters, until it gets good at predicting the next token.
  4. Teach it to chat. A second round of training turns it into an assistant. talkie's was done with help from a modern AI.

More parameters give a model more room to hold what it read; more tokens give it more to learn from. talkie is small on both counts, and it is also weak at reasoning a problem through.

The outside guesses come from an analyst who measured how much each model knows, and they cover the previous version of each model. They span a factor of ten, so read them as a sense of scale, not as facts. If they are roughly right, today's leading models are somewhere between 50 and 500 times talkie's size. The open model in the last row, released two years ago, is already about 30 times talkie's size.

Size is not the only gap. talkie read 260 billion tokens, all printed before 1931. Llama 3.1 read about 15 trillion, nearly 60 times as much. Google, Anthropic and OpenAI have not said how much their models read.

What is a token? Before a model reads anything, it cuts the text into tokens. A common word is usually one token; a rare word or an old-fashioned name is split into pieces. In English a token averages about three-quarters of a word, so talkie's 260 billion tokens come to roughly 200 billion words. To watch it happen, paste a sentence from your excerpt into the OpenAI Tokenizer or Tiktokenizer (no sign-in needed) and see which words get split.

05

Hallucination

Hallucination

A model predicts likely words. Unless it is searching the web, it is not looking anything up. When it does not know, it can still give a fluent, confident answer that is wrong. That is a hallucination.

It is more likely when

  • the model is small or read little, like talkie
  • the question assumes something false (watch for this in the Lincoln–Stowe demo next)
  • you ask for an exact quotation, a page number, a date or a count
  • the person or event is obscure, so little was written about it
  • you ask the model about itself

Large models hallucinate too, only more smoothly. Whenever any machine invents a name, date, quotation or source, note it on your card with the model and the question. In the discussion you will compare which voices invented the most, and on what kind of question.

One-minute test Ask your model, "How many parameters do you have?" Its maker has not published the number. A good answer says so; an exact figure stated as fact is made up.

Lincoln meets Stowe

Opening · 10 min

One meeting. Two versions, fourteen years apart.

Harriet Beecher Stowe, author of Uncle Tom's Cabin, met President Lincoln at the White House in December 1862. Neither of them wrote down what he said.

1897 · Annie Fields, Life and Letters of Harriet Beecher Stowe, p. 269

It was left for others to speak of Mrs. Stowe's interview with President Lincoln. Her daughter was told that when the President heard her name he seized her hand, saying, "Is this the little woman who made this great war?"

1911 · Her son and grandson, Harriet Beecher Stowe: The Story of Her Life, pp. 202–203

It was Mr. Seward who introduced her, and Mr. Lincoln rose awkwardly from his chair, saying, "Why, Mrs. Stowe, right glad to see you!" Then with a humorous twinkle in his eye, he said, "So you're the little woman who wrote the book that made this great war! Sit down, please," he added, as he seated himself once more before the fire […].
Compare the two
  • Who is the source of the story in 1897? Who is it in 1911?
  • What details does the 1911 version add?
  • Which version have you heard before, and why might that one have won?

Both passages are in full on the first page of your team packet.

Ask the machines

Opening · demo

Now ask the machines the same question

Your instructor runs these live. Watch whether either machine tells you how it knows.

To talkie
Did President Lincoln once greet Mrs. Harriet Beecher Stowe as the little woman who made the great war? What exactly did he say?
To the modern AI
Did President Lincoln once greet Harriet Beecher Stowe as "the little woman who wrote the book that made this great war"? How do we know? Name the earliest source for the quotation.

What historians found. The greeting first appeared in print in 1896, thirty-four years after the meeting, as a story told to Stowe's daughter. Lincoln left no record of it, and neither did Stowe. Daniel Vollaro traced every version and concluded that the words cannot be confirmed.

Today's job The anecdote grew more vivid as it moved away from the event. AI answers can do the same thing. Today you check them the way Vollaro checked this one.

Step 1 · talkie

12 min

Interview talkie

  • Open talkie-lm.com/chat.
  • Never mention a year or "1930," and never tell it what you expect.
  • Start with your team's questions below, then follow up in your own words.
  • Copy its most striking sentence word for word onto your card (②), and take a screenshot.
TeamStart with these
Team 1What sort of man was General Robert E. Lee? · What did General Lee say to his army when it surrendered? · Was Lee right to resign from the United States Army and fight for Virginia? · Should Americans honor General Lee? Why or why not?
Team 2What sort of man was General Ulysses S. Grant? · How did General Grant defeat General Lee? · Was Grant a good President? · What did Frederick Douglass think of General Grant?
Team 3What happened in the Southern States after the war, during Reconstruction? · Were the Reconstruction governments of the Southern States good or bad? · Should the freedmen have been given the vote? · What was the Ku Klux Klan, and what did it do?
Team 4What caused the war between the North and the South? · Did the South fight to keep slavery? · Upon what idea was the Confederate government founded? · Was secession lawful?
Every teamLast question: Who are the best writers to read on this subject? Note the names it gives, and whose names are missing.

Ask in plain words, the way a visitor might. A leading question gets you your own opinion back.

Listen for Does it say "the Rebellion" (the Northern name) or "the War between the States" (the Southern one)? Does it mention slavery without being asked? If talkie will not take your question, use the printed transcript for your team.

Step 3 · Today and a check

13 min

Ask what historians say now. Then check it against a book you have read.

  • Each of you sends prompt A in your own model. Write today's view in one line on your card (④).
  • Prompt A also asks about your team's course book. You have read it; the AI has only read about it. Does its summary match what you read? Where is it vague, or wrong?

13

Discussion

15 min

Why did the story change?

  • Whose books did talkie learn from? Whose writing is missing?
  • Du Bois wrote in 1910, inside talkie's world, and again in 1935, outside it. Did talkie sound like Du Bois, or like Dunning?
  • What did the modern AI learn from? Is it neutral, or is it simply 2026?
  • Which voice made up the most? Was it the smallest model, or the hardest kind of question?
What was happening before 1930
  • A defeat explained by numbers. Lee's farewell order (1865) and Early's speech (1872) said the South was beaten only by greater numbers and resources.
  • Reunion. White Northerners and Southerners honored each other's soldiers, and slavery slipped out of the story (Adams, 1907).
  • Reconstruction as a wrong done to the white South. A best-selling novel and a Columbia professor told the same story (Dixon, 1905; Dunning, 1907).
  • Control of the schoolbook. The United Daughters of the Confederacy pressed schools to teach that the South had not fought for slavery (Rutherford, 1914).
  • Counter-voices. Black writers kept slavery and freedom at the center (Douglass, 1881; Du Bois, 1910).
And after 1930, in your course books
  • Du Bois answered the Dunning School at book length in Black Reconstruction in America (1935).
  • Paul Buck's The Road to Reunion (1937) praised the reunion of North and South. David Blight's Race and Reunion (2001) argues that Black Americans paid for it.
  • Robert Penn Warren (1961) called the South's use of the war to excuse everything its "Great Alibi."

The historian of 2126

15 min

You are a historian in 2126

talkie is a source about 1930. The modern AI you used today is a source about 2026. Pick one answer it gave you and write one paragraph, about 120 words, as a historian a hundred years from now.

Start your paragraph with
In 2026, AI systems usually explained the Civil War by saying that ______. This tells us that the society of 2026 ______.

Answer at least one of these in your paragraph:

  • What could a 2026 AI not know, or not say?
  • Whose writing was it most likely trained on, and whose was left out?
  • What might a historian in 2126 find old-fashioned about it?
