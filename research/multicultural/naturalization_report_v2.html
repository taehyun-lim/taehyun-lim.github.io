Naturalization of marriage migrants in Korea: origin paths around 2011

Consolidated report v2 · updated 26 October 2026 (controls, GLOBALCIT) · National Multicultural Family Surveys 2015, 2018, 2021, 2024 · descriptive and associational; no causal effects identified

Table 1Table 2Figure 1Figure 2Table 3Table 4Table 5Appendix
Main findings
  • In absolute terms, only the Philippines and mainland China fell consistently after 2011 (duration-standardized rates down 4.5–7.1 points in all three surveys); Vietnamese, Korean Chinese and the other origins show no consistent change, and Taiwan’s rate rose on few events (Table 1B).
  • Against 2005–08, the Philippines diverges from Vietnam and Taiwan after 2011 in both surveys and under every inference method (Table 2A), and also with sex, birth cohort and entry age controlled or among women only (Table 2E); against 2009–10 the evidence is weak.
  • The Philippine relative decline matches mainland China's. Under GLOBALCIT's coding the Philippines has no loss rule since 2004, yet it falls even relative to origins that strip citizenship; the no-loss advantage rests on Vietnam alone (Tables 2B, 2D).
  • Adding children (dated before the event), union end, household income and own employment leaves the 2011 contrasts and the Philippine decline nearly unchanged (Tables 2C, 3B, 3D). Most divorced respondents naturalized before the union ended.
  • Later Philippine cohorts married more like Vietnamese cohorts (larger age gaps, more agency matches) yet naturalized far less; observed composition explains at most 18% of the decline, and at most 19% with all composition and family controls combined (Tables 4, 3A, 3D).
  • Philippine non-naturalized respondents plan to naturalize less, largely through “don't know”; the gap narrows from −13 to −3 pp by 2024. Philippine naturalized respondents rarely report keeping Philippine nationality (Table 5).

Key takeaways
  • Only the Philippines and mainland China fell consistently. Their duration-standardized annual rates dropped by 4.5–7.1 points, from 9–13% in 2005–08 to 4–6% in 2011–14, in all three surveys; the falls are significant with year clusters in 5 of 6 estimates (exception: Philippines in the 2018 survey, p = .093).
  • No consistent change elsewhere. For Korean Chinese, Vietnam, Japan, Thailand, Mongolia, Cambodia the standardized change lies between -2.8 and +3.0 pp; the only significant estimate among them is Mongolia in the 2015 survey (-1.8 pp, p = .023), not repeated in the other surveys. Vietnam's Δ is +1.9, -0.9, +1.2 pp (year-cluster p = .197, .720, .412); its 2015 increase is significant only in the respondent bootstrap and comes from long durations (six years or more), where few Vietnamese were at risk in 2005–08.
  • Taiwan rose (Δ +3.6, +1.3, +3.2 pp; year-cluster p = .022, .502, .011), but on only 85–127 events per survey.
  • Standardization matters for Vietnam. Its observed rate rose by +7.0 and +4.2 points (2015, 2021 surveys) because many Vietnamese arrived after 2005 and reached peak naturalization durations after 2011; holding the years-since-entry distribution fixed removes most of that rise.

Key takeaways
  • Relative, not absolute. Against 2005–08, Vietnam's odds of naturalizing changed 2.41 and 2.57 times as much as the Philippines' (2015, 2021 surveys) and Taiwan's 5.52 and 9.55 times; all four contrasts are significant (CR2 p ≤ .010). Against 2009–10 the evidence is weak in the 2021 survey (p = .131–.460).
  • The divergence comes from the Philippines falling, not Vietnam rising (Table 1B). Relative to the average origin, the Philippines (OR 0.55, 0.45) and mainland China (0.49, 0.56) are the two origins that fell (Table 2B).
  • The contrasts survive controls for children, union end, income and employment (Table 2C) and for sex, birth cohort and entry age (Table 2E). Among women only, Taiwan ÷ Philippines is about half as large (2.74, 5.30) because Taiwan's sample is half men; Vietnam ÷ Philippines does not change.
  • Origin nationality law does not explain the pattern: without Vietnam the no-loss advantage vanishes (1.03–1.06), it is never significant with origin clusters, and the Philippines falls even relative to loss origins (0.55–0.71) despite having no loss rule (Table 2D).

Notes on the added controls
  • Income. Simplified naturalization requires financial self-sufficiency, so household monthly income (four bands) and own employment and wage (four groups) were added. Both are measured at the survey, usually after naturalization, and can be consequences of it; they are robustness controls, not pre-naturalization measures. Household income is barely associated with naturalization within origin and years in Korea; own employment and wage show small and inconsistent associations (-2.5 to +4.2 pp across surveys, nine origins pooled).
  • Marital status. Most currently divorced or separated respondents naturalized before the union ended (table below). Those who did not naturalize before divorce are likely to have lost their status and left Korea, so current divorce partly reflects naturalization rather than causing it. The main specifications therefore use the dated ‘union ended before year t’ indicator; current marital status enters only as a sensitivity step.
  • Children. Co-resident children's ages give each child's birth year, so ‘child born by year t’ precedes the outcome. Births are strongly associated with naturalization (odds ratio 1.3–2.3; the least precise estimate is Philippines, 2018 survey: 1.26, 95% CI 0.88–1.79), more so for Vietnamese than Filipino women, but the share with a child within five years of entry is similar for the two origins and did not fall in later Philippine cohorts, so children do not explain the Philippine decline. Children living elsewhere are not observed.
  • Result. The 2011 origin contrasts (Table 2C) and the Philippine cohort decline (Table 3B) change little with these controls.

Key takeaways
  • Among women with a co-resident spouse, Philippine naturalization within five years fell by 40–49 points from the earliest to the latest entry cohort; the latest Philippine cohort is 19–30 points below Vietnam's.

Key takeaways
  • Later Philippine cohorts look more like Vietnamese cohorts: the husband's age gap grew from 8.7 to 14.4 years (Vietnam 17.3), agency matches from 23% to 37% (Vietnam 47%), and religious-organization matches fell from 35% to 1%.

Key takeaways
